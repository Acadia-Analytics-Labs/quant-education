# On The Calculus of Value by Howard Marks

Howard Marks (b. 1946) is an American investor, writer, and co-founder of Oaktree Capital Management, one of the world’s leading investment firms specializing in alternative assets and distressed debt. Known for his insightful client memos, Marks is widely respected for his clear thinking on risk, market cycles, and investment psychology. We summarize here his recent memo, The Calculus of Value, published August 13, 2025.

In this memo, Marks discusses the state of public investment asset values in the United States, updated from his previous memo *On Bubble Watch* published in early January.  He begins by outlining the distinction between an asset's price and its value.  An asset's value --- what it is worth at a point in time --- has no objective value, but Marks conceptualizes it as being derived from its *fundamentals*.  Fundamentals encompass many things, including its current and/or future earning value, the skill of management, and the strength of its balance sheet.  Value is directly derived from earning power, which is in turn derived from the asset's fundamentals.  In general, the earning power of a company is greater than the sum total of the earning power of each individual asset, which can be tangible (such as the number of buildings owned) or intangible (such as human knowledge or reputation).

He contrasts value with price, which is the amount a buyer is willing to pay to obtain something.  Marks notes that he was taught in business school that the right price for an asset is the discounted present value of its future cash flows or earnings; put differently, an asset is worth what its future earnings are worth today, once you've accounted for time and risk.  However, in the real world, the discounted rate is often primarily governed by investor sentiment, or the subjective beliefs about what the asset and its earning power are actually worth.  When investors are by and large optimistic, prices will rise, whereas a negative general sentiment will cause prices to fall.  The relationship between an asset's price and value is known as **valuation**.

Marks writes that one can think of value as holding a magnetic influence on price.  Namely, if a price is above value, future prices will presumably be lower than the original.  The converse is also true: if an asset is undervalued, price movements will tend to be upward.  When an asset's price increases astronomically, the resulting effect is a bubble; similarly, a dramatic fall in price is known as a crash.  Overall, it is logically sound to assume that price and value will converge towards one another in the long term, but Marks hazards against betting that this will occur soon.  

Thus, one can think about the process of valuation in terms of a **calculus of value**, a logical, rule-governed way of thinking about the relationship between value and price.  However, and this is key: investment psychology is not necessarily rational, and in fact, is often volatile.  He outlines a few key tenets to the calculus of value, including that "a good investment is one in which the price is right for what the value turns out to be," and emphasizing that (in theory) the relationship of price to value has a strong, predictive impact on investment performances, in the sense that high valuations (i.e. overvaluations) will presage low returns and vice versa.  Again, this effect is probabilistic and not expected to necessarily occur on short timescales.  Generally, returns can be thought of as stemming from (a) a change in the asset's fundamentals (i.e. business growth) or (b) a change in the asset's valuation (i.e. an asset becomes temporarily more popular with investors, thus increasing its price).

So how, then, do equity investors measure the fairness of a given price at a given time? Enter the p/e (price-to-earnings) ratio, a valuation metric for stocks that is the ratio of its share price to its earnings per share.  It can be calculated for single stocks, groups of stocks, or ETFs.  It can be used in comparison to other assets' p/e ratios to assess whether an asset is overpriced, underpriced, or fairly priced.  For context, the S&P 500's p/e ratio towards the end of last year was 23, historically above average.  More information about the p/e ratio can be found [here](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/p/price-earningsratio.asp).  Marks remarks that the particularly high market valuation is potentially worrisome.

In his concluding remarks, Marks outlines a defense readiness condition he calls INVESTCON, similar to DEFCON, for investors to make decisions according to corresponding `threat' levels in the market.  In decreasing order of drasticness, 
1. Go short
2. Eliminate all holdings
3. Trim defensive holdings
4. Sell off remaining aggressive holdings
5. Reduce aggressive holdings and increase defensive holdings
6. Stop buying
