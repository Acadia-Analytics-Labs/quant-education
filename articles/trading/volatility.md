# Measuring Market Risk

Every investment involves some level of risk. Since no one can predict exactly how the markets will move, investors have developed several tools to measure and understand risk that come with certain stocks. 

The three most common measures of market risk are **volatility**, the **CBOE Volatility Index (VIX)**, and **beta**. Each measures a different type of market risk and helps investors to better understand how an investment may behave under different market conditions. 

Understanding these concepts can help investors build portfolios that match their financial goals and risk tolerance. 

## Volatility 

Volatility measures how much the price of an asset changes over time. A highly volatile investment experiences large price swings, while a low-volatility investment tends to move more gradually. 

Volatility also measures both upward and downward price movements. A stock that frequently gains 5% and loses 5% the next day is considered more volatile than a stock that consistently changes by only 1% each day, even if both stocks earn similar long-term returns. 

Higher volatility generally means greater uncertainty because prices fluctuate more dramatically over short periods. Lower volatility suggests that prices tend to be more stable and predictable. However, higher volatility does not necessarily mean an investment is bad. Instead it simply indicates that returns are likely to vary more widely over time. 

### Standard Deviation 

Standard deviation is the most widely used measure of volatility. It measures how spread out returns are from their average value. 

A low standard deviation means that returns stay relatively close to the average. A high standard deviation means that returns will vary significantly from the average. 

The formula for standard deviation is: 

$$
\sigma=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(R_i-\bar{R})^2}{n-1}}
$$

Where:

* **σ** = standard deviation
* **R<sub>i</sub>** = each individual return
* **R̄** = average return
* **n** = number of observations

For example: 

* **Portfolio A** has an annual return of 5% and a standard deviation of 4%  
* **Portfolio B** has an annual return of 15% and a standard deviation of 35%

Although portfolio B has a higher expected annual return, it also experiences much larger price swings. This means that one year portfolio B could lose 20% and portfolio A could have a return of 9%. Investors that are seeking higher returns must often be willing to tolerate greater volatility. 

### Realized vs. Implied Volatility

Realized and implied volatility are the two major ways that investors think about volatility. 

#### Realized Volatility 

Also known as historical volatility, realized volatility looks backward. It measures how much prices moved during a previous period. 

Investors use realized volatility to:

* Compare investments  
* Study historical market behavior  
* Estimate portfolio risk 

#### Implied Volatility 

Implied volatility looks forward. Rather than using past prices, it is derived from option prices and reflects how much investors expert prices to move in the future. 

When option prices become more expensive, implied volatility usually rises because traders expect larger future price movement. 

Unlike historical volatility, implied volatility represents market expectations rather than actual outcomes. 

## The VIX

One of the best known measures of implied volatility is the CBOE Volatility Index (VIX). 

The VIX estimates the expected volatility of the S\&P 500 Index over the next 30 days using prices of the S\&P 500 index options. 

Even though it is often called the "fear index," the VIX does not predict whether stock prices will go up or down. Instead, it measures the level of expected market volatility.

### Interpretation 

A higher VIX value indicates that investors expect larger market swings. 

Typical ranges for the VIX are:

* **Below 15**: Low expected volatility with generally calm markets   
* **15-25**: Normal market conditions   
* **25-35**: Elevated uncertainty  
* **Above 35**: High expected volatility and increased investor fear

During major market events, the VIX can rise to extremely high levels. For example, during the 2008 financial crisis and the Covid-19 market crash, the VIX briefly rose to above 80\. 

### What causes the VIX to Rise?

Several events can increase expected market volatility, including:

* Economic recessions  
* Unexpected inflation data  
* Central bank policy announcements  
* Geopolitical conflicts  
* Financial crises  
* Corporate earnings surprises

As uncertainty from one of these events increases, demand for options often rises. Higher option demand pushes up option prices, increasing implied volatility and therefore the VIX.

## Beta

Beta measures how sensitive an investment is to movements in the overall market. This is different from volatility, which measures how much an investment moves on its own. 

Beta compares an asset’s returns to those of a benchmark. The most common is the S\&P 500 which is assigned a beta of 1.0.

The formula for beta is:

$$
\beta=\frac{\text{Cov}(R_i,R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)}
$$

Where: 

* **β** = beta
* **Cov(R<sub>i</sub>, R<sub>m</sub>)** = covariance between the investment's returns and the market's returns
* **Var(R<sub>m</sub>)** = variance of the market's returns
* **R<sub>i</sub>** = returns of the investment
* **R<sub>m</sub>** = returns of the market

### Interpretation

Beta indicates how much an investment tends to move relative to the overall market. 

* **β** **\= 0**: The assets return is not correlated with the market   
* **β between 0 and 1**: The asset moves in the same direction as the market but is less volatile  
* **β \= 1**: The asset tends to move in line with the market   
* **β \> 1**: The asset moves in the directions as the market but is more volatile  
* **Negative β**: The asset moves in the opposite direction as the market 

### Example

Suppose the S\&P 500 increases by 10%.

* A stock with a beta of 1.5 would be expected to gain approximately 15%.  
* If the S\&P 500 instead declined by 10%, the same stock would be expected to lose approximately 15%.

Now suppose another company has a beta of \-0.5.

* If the market gains 10%, the company would be expected to decline by roughly 5%.  
* If the market falls 10%, the company would be expected to gain approximately 5%.

These are statistical expectations based on historical relationships. Actual returns can differ significantly because beta measures average historical sensitivity rather than providing exact predictions.

### High-Beta vs. Low-Beta Stocks

Examples of high-beta stocks include:

* Technology companies  
* Growth stocks  
* Semiconductor manufactures  
* Small-cap companies

These stocks tend to outperform during bull markets but often experience larger declines during market downturns. 

Examples of low-beta stocks include:

* Utility companies  
* Consumer staples  
* Healthcare providers  
* Large defensive companies

These stocks typically experience smaller price swings and may hold up better during periods of market stress. 

### Limitations of Beta

Although beta is widely used by investors, it does have several limitations.

* Beta is based on historical data and may not predict future behavior  
* Beta measures only market related (systematic) risk, not company specific risks such as poor management or product failures  
* Beta can change over time as a company’s business model or industry evolves  
* During periods of market stress, relationships between stocks and the overall market may shift significantly

These limitations mean that investors use beta along with other risk measures than solely relying on beta to assess risk.  

## Conclusion

Volatility, the VIX, and beta all provide different perspectives on investment risk. 

Volatility measures how much of an asset’s price fluctuates, helping investors understand the magnitude of past or expected price movements. The VIX reflects the market's expectations for future volatility by using option prices on the S\&P 500\. This makes it a widely followed indicator of market uncertainty. Beta measures how strongly an investment tends to move relative to the overall market, which provides insight into its exposure to systematic risk. 

There is no single measure that provides a complete picture of market risk. Investors consider all three of these measures together along with other factors such as diversification, valuation, and investment objectives, to make more informed portfolio decisions.
