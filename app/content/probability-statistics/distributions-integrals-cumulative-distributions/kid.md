# Lining Up the Whole Class 📏

Your teacher asks everyone to line up shortest to tallest.

Right away you can see two completely different things about your class, and they have nothing to do with each other.

The first is **where the middle is**. If you walk to the person standing halfway down the line, you get a feel for a "typical" height. Grown-ups call that the **mean**, or the average.

The second is **how stretched out the line is**. Maybe everybody is nearly the same height and the line is a tight little clump. Or maybe there's a really short kid at one end, a really tall kid at the other, and everyone spread out in between. Same middle, totally different line! That stretchiness has a name too: the **spread**, or the **variance**.

## The hill

If you drew a picture of the line — tall where lots of kids are bunched together, low where hardly anyone is — you'd get a shape like a hill. That hill is called a **density**. It doesn't tell you how many kids are at *exactly* 4 feet 3 inches, because nobody is at exactly anything if you measure carefully enough. It tells you how **crowded** each stretch of the line is.

To find out how many kids are between 4 and 5 feet, you take that slice of the hill and measure how much hill is sitting there. More hill in a slice means more kids. And if you measure the *whole* hill, you get everybody — 100% of the class.

## Counting from the start

Here's the last trick, and it's the useful one. Instead of asking about a slice in the middle, start at the very shortest kid and keep counting as you walk right.

*How many kids are shorter than 4 feet?* Maybe 3 of them. *Shorter than 5 feet?* Now 18. *Shorter than 6 feet?* Everybody.

That running total only ever goes **up** — you can never un-count a kid. It starts at nobody and ends at everybody. Grown-ups call it the **cumulative** count.

And here's the neat part: once you have the running total, any slice is just subtraction. Kids between 4 and 5 feet? That's the 18 up to 5 feet, minus the 3 up to 4 feet. Fifteen kids. No hill-measuring required. 📏
