# The Giant Class Pizza Order 🍕

Imagine your whole class wants pizza. You've each got **\$5**. On your own, \$5 buys you one sad, plain slice. But if all thirty of you drop your \$5 into one big jar, suddenly there's **\$150** — enough for a huge order with pepperoni, veggie, cheese, and even the fancy one with pineapple on it.

Nobody has to pick just one pizza and hope it's good. Everybody gets a little bit of *everything*.

A **mutual fund** works exactly like that jar. Lots of people put their money in together. Then one grown-up whose whole job is picking investments — the **fund manager** — uses the big pile to buy little pieces of hundreds of different companies. You own a slice of the whole order.

## Waiting until the end of the day

Here's the one weird rule. Some investments you can buy and sell any second, like trading cards at recess. A mutual fund isn't like that. Everyone who buys or sells today gets the **same** price, and nobody knows what it is until the market closes and someone adds up what all the pizzas are worth.

That number has a name: the **NAV**, short for *net asset value*. It just means "add up everything the fund owns, subtract what it owes, and split it evenly by the number of shares."

## The crumbs

The grown-up doing the ordering doesn't work for free. Each year the fund keeps a tiny sliver of your money — maybe fifty cents for every \$100. That sliver is the **expense ratio**. It sounds like nothing, but it gets taken *every single year*, so the smaller it is, the more pizza stays yours. 🍕
