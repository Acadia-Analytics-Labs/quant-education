# Who Coaches the Economy? 🏟️

The economy is like one **giant team game**. Millions of players are all moving at once: you buying pizza, your parents going to work, shops selling sneakers, factories making phones. Nobody is in charge of all of it.

So here's a puzzle that grown-ups have argued about for **hundreds of years**: does this huge game need a *coach*? And if it does, what should the coach actually do — stay on the bench, or run onto the field?

It turns out there isn't just one answer. There are lots of famous "coaches," and each one brought a different **playbook**.

## Two big playbooks

Most of the arguing comes down to **two very different game plans**:

- 🧑‍🏫 **"Let them play."** Coach Smith believed that when everyone chases what *they* want — the baker bakes bread, you spend your \$5 allowance on candy — the whole team somehow ends up better off, like magic. He called it the *invisible hand*. His advice: coach, stay on the bench and don't mess it up.
- 📣 **"Step in when we're losing."** Coach Keynes saw the team freeze up during the Great Depression and *stay* frozen. His advice: when everybody stops spending at the same time, the coach should jump in, spend, and get the ball rolling again.

## The other coaches

- 🍪 **Coach Friedman** cared most about the *snack supply* — real name: the money supply. Print way too much money and prices go crazy (that's **inflation**); too little and the game grinds to a halt.
- 🧠 **Coach Kahneman** noticed the players are *human*. They panic, they get greedy, they copy their friends. You can't plan the game as if everyone is a calm little calculator.

## The real secret

Here's the grown-up truth: **no coach has the perfect playbook.** Smith was right that free players are powerful and creative. Keynes was right that a scared team sometimes needs a push. Friedman was right to watch the snacks.

Today's smartest thinkers don't pick just one coach — they keep the best play from each and switch depending on whether the team is winning or losing. 🏆
