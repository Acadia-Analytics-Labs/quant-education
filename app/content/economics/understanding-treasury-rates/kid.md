# The Government's IOU 🏛️

Imagine the government needs money to build a bridge. It asks you: *"Can I borrow \$100? I'll pay you back later — and give you a little extra for waiting."*

You say yes, and it hands you a fancy note — an **IOU**. That note is basically what a **Treasury bond** is: a promise from the government to pay you back, plus interest along the way.

## The seesaw

Here's the secret every grown-up bond trader knows. Your old note still pays the same \$5 forever. It can't change. So the only thing that *can* change is its **price** when you try to sell it.

- When new notes pay **more**, nobody wants your old low-paying note. Its price **drops**.
- When new notes pay **less**, everyone wants your old high-paying note. Its price **jumps**.

Interest and price sit on opposite ends of a seesaw. Push one up, the other goes down.


```ascii
   interest UP                 price DOWN
        \                         /
         \___________/\__________/
                   (seesaw)
         ___________  \__________
        /                         \
   interest DOWN               price UP
```


## Why the "extra" changes

That yearly \$5 is really about **trust and patience**. If prices in the shops are rising fast (that's called inflation), your \$5 buys less candy next year — so people demand *bigger* payments to keep lending. If everyone is scared and wants a super-safe place to park money, they'll happily lend for a *smaller* payment, because the government almost never breaks its promise.

So the whole game is: an old IOU's price seesaws against interest rates, and interest rates move with how patient, scared, or worried about rising prices everyone feels. Lend, wait, collect — and remember the seesaw. ⚖️🏆
